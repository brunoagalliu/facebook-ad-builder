# Offer Framing and Calls to Action

Even a strong hook and body die at the finish line if the action feels risky, slow, or vague. Drawn from Sugarman's and Halbert's offer-construction work.

## Making the offer feel low-risk
- **Risk reversal** — guarantees, free trials, "pay nothing today," money-back framing. State it plainly and near the CTA, not buried earlier in the copy — the reader's hesitation peaks right before they click.
- **Offer stacking** — bundling a bonus, a discount, and a guarantee reads as more generous than a single discount of equivalent value; name each piece rather than a vague "plus more."
- **Reason-why** — a stated reason for the deal (clearance, anniversary, limited batch, introductory pricing) makes the offer more believable than a deal with no explanation. Unexplained discounts read as suspicious to sophisticated audiences.

## CTA construction
- The CTA is an instruction, not a suggestion: "Get Yours Now," "Claim Your Spot," "Start Free Trial" — active verb, first-person-adjacent, immediate.
- Restate the core benefit or urgency in or right next to the CTA where format allows — the CTA is the last thing read before deciding, and it's the cheapest place to reinforce the "why now."
- Avoid soft, non-committal CTAs ("Learn More," "See Options") for a direct-response ad unless the funnel genuinely requires an information step first — a direct-response ad should ask for the direct-response action (buy, claim, start), not a research action.
- Keep CTAs short and unambiguous — this is not the place for cleverness; the reader needs to know exactly what happens when they tap.

## Common failure mode
An ad with a strong hook and body but a generic, low-commitment CTA leaves all the built urgency stranded with nowhere to go — the CTA has to cash the check the rest of the copy wrote. If the hook promised urgency, the CTA must demand action, not invite consideration.
