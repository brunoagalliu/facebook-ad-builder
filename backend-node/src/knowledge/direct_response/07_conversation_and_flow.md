# Entering the Conversation and the Slippery Slide

Drawn from Robert Collier's "enter the conversation already taking place in the customer's mind" and Sugarman's "slippery slide." Both concern momentum — getting the first line believed and every line after it read.

## Enter the conversation (Collier)

The strongest opening line doesn't introduce a new idea — it voices the exact thought the reader is already having, so precisely that it reads like their own inner monologue rather than a pitch. "New Miracle Cream Fights Wrinkles" announces a product; "Tired of covering the same lines every morning?" continues a thought the reader was already thinking. The test for a hook or headline: could this sentence plausibly be something the reader said to themselves five minutes ago? If it reads as the brand introducing itself, it's arriving as an interruption, not a continuation — rewrite it from the reader's internal voice, not the marketer's external one.

## The slippery slide (Sugarman)

Once the opening line is read, its only job — and the job of every line after it — is to get the *next* line read. No sentence should be a resting point. Practically: keep sentences short, end lines on a small open loop rather than a closed thought (a detail promised but not yet delivered), and avoid fully resolving an idea until the reader has been pulled one line further. A body copy block that reads as a series of complete, self-contained statements will lose momentum even if each individual line is well-written; one that keeps forward pull until the CTA will carry the reader through.

## How these work together

Collier gets the first line believed by making it feel like the reader's own thought. Sugarman keeps every line after it moving so that belief doesn't stall out before reaching the offer. A hook that nails identification but is followed by static, fully-resolved sentences still loses the reader partway down — and a well-paced body copy block built on a hook that reads like a brand announcement never gets the reader to start sliding in the first place. Both have to be true.

## Practical rule for this engine

Check the opening line against Collier's test (does this sound like the reader's own thought, not our pitch) and check the line-to-line transitions against Sugarman's test (does each line create a reason to read the next one, or does it let the reader's attention settle). Fix the opening line first — a perfect slide starting from a dead hook still fails.
