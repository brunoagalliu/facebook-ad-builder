# Reason-Why and Scientific Proof

Drawn from Claude Hopkins' *Scientific Advertising*. Where `03_psychological_triggers.md` covers social/emotional levers, this covers the specific mechanism of belief: why a reader accepts a claim as true instead of dismissing it as advertising.

## An unexplained claim is an advertising claim; a claim with a reason is a fact

Readers have a built-in discount rate for anything that sounds like a marketer's assertion. "Clinically proven to reduce joint pain" is a claim. "Reduces joint pain by targeting the specific enzyme that causes cartilage inflammation" is a claim *with a mechanism* — and mechanisms read as facts, not pitches, because they're specific enough to be falsifiable. Whenever this engine states a benefit, it should also give the reason the benefit is true: an ingredient, a process, a policy, an origin story — something concrete the outcome is attached to, not the outcome floating on its own.

## Specificity is itself a form of proof

Hopkins' core observation, still true in performance ad copy: exact numbers, named specifics, and testable details read as true, while round numbers and superlatives read as advertising. "Lose weight fast" is dismissed on sight; "Drop 8lbs in the first 2 weeks" is evaluated on its merits, because the specificity signals the claim was measured, not invented. This is the same discipline as `00_doctrine.md`'s "47% smaller in 6 weeks beats revolutionary results," applied specifically to *why* it works: the number functions as evidence, not just as color.

## Sampling as the ultimate proof

Hopkins considered a free sample or trial the strongest form of persuasion available, because it removes the need for the reader to trust the claim at all — they can verify it themselves at no risk. When the offer includes a trial, guarantee, or sample (see `04_offer_and_cta.md`'s risk reversal), reason-why copy should point directly at it as the resolution to any remaining doubt: "don't take our word for it — try it yourself for 30 days," not just stating the guarantee as a policy detail.

## Practical rule for this engine

Never let a benefit claim stand unexplained. If the product description or brand inputs supply a real mechanism, ingredient, or process, use it as the reason-why. If they don't supply one explicitly, infer the most plausible, specific-sounding mechanism consistent with the product description rather than falling back to an unsupported superlative — a stated (even simple) reason will always out-convert a bare claim.
