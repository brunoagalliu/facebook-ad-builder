# The Big Idea and Craft Standards

Drawn from David Ogilvy's discipline of research-first, respect-driven salesmanship. This is a quality filter applied after a variation is drafted, not a generation technique on its own.

## The big idea test

Ogilvy: an ad needs one idea distinctive enough that a reader would remember it and repeat it to a friend — without a big idea, an ad is just decorated boilerplate that will pass unnoticed regardless of how polished the writing is. For this engine, "big idea" means a specific angle or claim, not a structural framework — running the same generic claim through a different body-copy structure (PAS vs. bullets) is not variation, it's the same idea wearing different clothes. Real variation comes from a different angle (see `06_angle_and_identification.md`), a different reason-why (see `08_reason_why_and_proof.md`), or a different trigger — not from restructuring the same sentence.

## Respect for the reader

Ogilvy's standard: the reader is not a fool to be tricked, they're someone whose intelligence and time deserve a real argument. Curiosity hooks with no payoff, claims that don't survive a second read, and cheap "you won't believe this" framing all cost trust the moment the reader notices the gap between the pitch and the substance — and a reader who feels tricked once discounts everything that follows in the ad, including the parts that were true. Craft, wit, and a strong hook are good; using them to disguise a weak or absent claim is not — see `00_doctrine.md`'s clarity-over-cleverness rule, which this reinforces at the level of trust rather than just readability.

## Research before the line

Ogilvy insisted on knowing the product cold before writing a single word, because generic copy is what happens when a writer doesn't actually know what makes this product different. For this engine: every claim in a variation should be traceable to something in the actual brand/product/profile/campaign inputs provided — a real detail, a real audience pain point, a real offer term — not a category-generic claim that could be dropped unchanged into a competitor's ad for a similar product.

## Practical rule for this engine

Before finalizing a variation, check it against two questions: (1) is there one specific, memorable idea here, or is this just a familiar claim in new packaging, and (2) is every claim in it actually anchored to something in this specific brand/product/audience/offer, or could it be copy-pasted into an unrelated competitor's ad without changing a word? A variation that fails either check needs a new angle, not a rewrite of the same one.
