# Rhythm and Cadence

Drawn from Herschell Gordon Lewis's *On the Art of Writing Copy*. Concerns how copy reads at the sentence level, independent of which framework or trigger it's built on.

## The read-aloud test

Copy that stumbles when read aloud will stumble in the reader's head too, even in silent reading — the brain still processes rhythm. Vary sentence length deliberately: a short, punchy sentence lands harder right after a slightly longer one that built it up, and a string of same-length sentences reads as monotone regardless of how good each individual line is. Avoid clause-stacking (multiple qualifying phrases in one sentence) in short-form ad copy — by the time the reader parses the clauses, the momentum from `07_conversation_and_flow.md`'s slippery slide has already stalled.

## Positive framing, with one exception

Describing what the reader gains or becomes generally outperforms describing only what they avoid — "walk pain-free" pulls harder than "stop suffering" as a default. This is a general-copy rule, not a blanket one: when the active technique is loss aversion specifically (`03_psychological_triggers.md`), negative framing of the cost of inaction *is* the point, and softening it into positive language there would undercut the trigger being used. Match the framing to the trigger being deployed rather than applying either rule by default everywhere.

## Practical rule for this engine

Read each draft variation at natural speaking pace before finalizing it. Any line that requires a re-read to parse — because it's clause-stacked, too long, or awkwardly ordered — should be shortened or split, even if the content is otherwise correct. A technically accurate sentence that reads awkwardly will lose more readers than a slightly simpler one that flows.
